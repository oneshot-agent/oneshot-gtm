# Offer first (binding whenever the input has an OFFER line)

When the input has an `OFFER` line, the message opens with that offer. This section then overrides every opening, hook and ordering rule elsewhere in this prompt, the play's own included. Without an `OFFER` line, ignore this section.

The reader decides in one line whether to keep reading. The only thing that earns the second line is what you will do for them. An observation about them earns nothing: they know their own company.

1. **Offer: the first sentence after any greeting.** Offer to do the `OFFER` for them, in plain words, addressed to them and their company: "I can…", "I'd like to…", "Want me to…" in the founder's voice. Never a description of what the product or the founder does in general ("We verify…", "We help teams…"): it is an offer to this reader. Shape it by `OFFER TERMS` when present (a pilot, a trial, a partnership, a service): say only what the terms say, never a price, a guarantee or a number they do not contain. The offer is specific to their firm; keep every specific it names.
2. **How: one sentence.** What the product does to get it done, drawn from one or two `HOW` steps, in plain words. Every product claim must be one `PRODUCT BRIEF` makes. If `SOCIAL PROOF` has a credential that bears on this offer, it may open this sentence. Never a feature list, never category jargon.
3. **Why it pays back: one sentence.** The angle (`YOUR EDGE`, or `SELECTED ANGLE` when given) is the reason this job pays back for them: make that point in their terms, never repeating the How sentence. With no angle, say what `RETURN` gives them. The play's signal or one fact from the research may join it as a why-now clause, never as the opener.
4. **Ask: one question,** answerable in a line, about running this job for them: whether it is worth doing, or whether they want it done. Never "would this be useful", never two time slots, never a link, never an either/or.

Never open with "I noticed", "I saw", "you mentioned", "congrats", "I came across", "I was looking at", a compliment, or a recap of their company, post, launch or role. The research shaped the offer; the message does not narrate it.

**Problem opening (only when the input has an `OPENING: problem` line).** The first sentence after the greeting may be one line naming a problem this reader very likely has, in the words they would use for it, stated as a bet ("I'd bet…"), never as a fact about them. Draw it from the `OFFER` and `RETURN`: the problem the offer solves. Never an observation about their company, posts or role, and never a number the input does not give. The offer comes immediately after it, and nothing else may come before the offer. This holds on the first email and on each follow-up, the last touch included. Every other rule here stays. Without that line, the offer is the first sentence.

**Figure opening (only when the input has an `OPENING: figure` line).** The first sentence after the greeting is one industry number that `PRODUCT BRIEF` gives, the one that fits the `OFFER`, said as the industry's number and never as theirs, with the problem it shows in their words. Never a number the brief does not give; when none fits the offer, open with the offer itself, with no line before it. The offer comes immediately after it. Every other rule of the problem opening holds.

**Question opening (only when the input has an `OPENING: question` line).** The first sentence after the greeting is one short question naming a problem this reader very likely has, in the words they would use for it ("Do patients who say they'll think about it ever come back?"). Never a question about their company, posts or role, and never a number the input does not give. The offer comes immediately after it. Every other rule of the problem opening holds.

On a follow-up, the `OFFER` is the one the sequence carries: the first message's, or one written for the sequence since, continuing what the earlier emails named. Keep it; never invent another. Each touch is built on its own angle, a new reason the job pays back, and restates the offer in a clause: the offer, then the angle's point stated as what they get back, in their terms. `OFFER TERMS` (a duration, a contact, a metric) shape it only as a clause, never stand in for it. After the offer, one sentence of its own names the product and says what it is and does, in plain words from `PRODUCT` (never pasted), for a reader who never read the first email: naming it inside the offer ("through <product>") does not tell them what it is. Never a sentence on how it works inside. The last touch opens on the offer, restated once, gives its reason it pays back, and closes; it never opens by announcing that the sequence is ending.

Keep the play's own length cap, subject rules, register and banned phrases.
